# The 2008 effect: crisis-era lender losses, mergers, failures, and absorption

This file looks at institutions in the all-lenders HMDA ranking data that were visibly affected by the 2007-2009 mortgage crisis, especially the lenders whose mortgage footprint disappeared, shrank sharply, or ended up inside a larger successor through failure, acquisition, or restructuring.

The table is not a complete legal merger database. It is a practical market-map: the left side is what appears in the HMDA ranking data, and the successor column shows where that lender or banking operation ended up when there is a clear public crisis-era event.

## Main takeaway

The 2008 shock did not affect every lender in the same way. The clearest pattern is that very large bank/thrift platforms were absorbed by stronger banks, while specialized subprime and nonprime mortgage companies often vanished from the ranking table or fell from top-tier ranks to negligible volume. This is why post-crisis mortgage lending became more concentrated among survivors, government-channel specialists, large banks, and later nonbank originators.

## What happened by category

### 1. Failed or seized depository institutions

- **Washington Mutual** is the cleanest example. It was still a giant HMDA lender in 2007, ranking #6 by loans funded in this dataset with 261,476 funded loans. It then disappears as a standalone lender after the September 25, 2008 seizure. The FDIC states that JPMorgan Chase acquired Washington Mutual’s banking operations from the FDIC receivership.
- **IndyMac Bank** ranked #11 in 2007 with 141,524 funded loans. It failed in July 2008. In the HMDA data it reappears only as a much smaller residual footprint by 2009, showing the difference between a going-concern top lender and a post-failure runoff/successor situation.
- **Downey Savings and Loan** was a mid-sized but meaningful California thrift lender in 2007 and 2008. The FDIC says it was closed in November 2008 and U.S. Bank acquired deposit accounts; the FDIC page also states all deposits and loans were transferred to U.S. Bank.

### 2. Distressed bank acquisitions

- **Countrywide** is the most direct mortgage-origination shock. In 2007, Countrywide Home Loans and Countrywide Bank FSB together funded more than 912,000 loans in this dataset. Bank of America completed the purchase of Countrywide on July 1, 2008. The standalone Countrywide Home Loans name collapses in the table by 2008, while Countrywide Bank FSB remains large in 2008 and smaller in 2009 before disappearing into the Bank of America structure.
- **Wachovia** shows a similar banking-platform absorption. Wachovia Bank NA, Wachovia Mortgage, and Wachovia Mortgage FSB were all visible in 2007-2009. Wells Fargo acquired Wachovia in 2008, and the standalone Wachovia names fade after that.
- **National City Bank** ranked #7 in 2007 and #11 in 2008 in this dataset. PNC completed its acquisition of National City effective December 31, 2008; after that, National City disappears as a standalone top lender while PNC continues as the successor platform.

### 3. Subprime and nonprime mortgage-company exits

- **First Franklin, GreenPoint, Option One, Argent, Accredited, Fremont Investment & Loan, EquiFirst, Saxon, Bear Stearns Residential Mortgage, and Lehman Brothers Bank FSB** illustrate the collapse of the private-label/subprime ecosystem. Many were ranked inside or near the top 100 in 2007, then either vanished immediately or fell sharply by 2008-2009.
- These names are important because their disappearance created market space. Some of that space went to large banks for a period, some went to FHA/VA-oriented lenders and servicers, and later much of the origination growth shifted toward nonbank mortgage companies with different funding models.

### 4. Survived but materially restructured or shrank

- **GMAC Mortgage / Ally / ResCap lineage** did not simply vanish in 2008, but the HMDA data shows a severe change. GMAC Mortgage LLC ranked #29 in 2007 and #22 in 2008, then fell to #467 in 2009 and kept sliding after that. This is a good example of a lender that remained visible but no longer operated with its pre-crisis mortgage footprint.
- **Merrill Lynch mortgage entities** also show post-crisis absorption into the Bank of America orbit after the broader Merrill transaction, though the exact HMDA names vary by legal entity.

## Reading the market through this lens

The 2008 effect was both a **collapse** and a **transfer of capacity**. The collapse happened among lenders tied heavily to subprime, nonprime, private-label securitization, option ARM, and wholesale channels. The transfer happened when deposits, branches, servicing, or banking operations moved into JPMorgan Chase, Wells Fargo, Bank of America, PNC, U.S. Bank, and other stronger successor institutions.

This matters for the UWM/nonbank story because the crisis cleared out a large part of the old mortgage-company universe. UWM did not simply “take” Countrywide’s or WaMu’s loans in a direct one-to-one way. But the disappearance of those firms changed the competitive field: fewer legacy subprime players, tighter bank credit, more government-channel lending, and eventually more room for scaled nonbank originators and wholesale lenders to gain share as the market normalized.
