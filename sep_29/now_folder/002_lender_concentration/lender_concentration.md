# Lender concentration

Each row is one filing institution, counted by the applications it received. That is the same definition as the yearly outcomes note: the loan was made, approved but not taken, denied, withdrawn, or left incomplete. Loans a lender bought from someone else are left out.

From 2007 through 2017, the name is the one on that year's HMDA filing. From 2018 on, institutions are tracked by legal-entity identifier, and the name is the one on the 2025 filing for that same entity. Quicken Loans therefore shows up as Rocket Mortgage from 2018 onward. One 2014 name was missing from that year's lookup; the same respondent id is Ditech Financial LLC in 2015–2017, so that name is used for 2014.

## Is it a Pareto market?

Yes, in the practical sense. A small share of lenders takes in most of the applications. It is steeper than the textbook 80/20 picture, and the top 10 are only the tip of that group.

The top 10 are always 10 institutions. In 2025 they are 10 out of 4,779 lenders, about 0.2% of the list, and they received 22.5% of applications. Half of all applications that year sat with 47 lenders. Four out of five sat with 358 lenders, about 7.5% of the list.

Across the whole series, the top 10 received between 20% and 35% of applications. Reaching 80% takes a few hundred lenders, roughly 3% to 9% of whoever filed that year. Twenty percent of lenders would be a much longer list. The heavy part of the market is a few hundred firms, and inside that group the first 10 are large but do not hold the market by themselves.

## Insights

1. Half the market is a short list. Depending on the year, 29 to 66 lenders received half of all applications. In 2021, the most concentrated recent year on this measure, 40 lenders were enough. In 2025 it was 47.

2. The top 10 are the storefront names, and they are about a quarter of the business, not most of it. Their share peaked near 35% in 2009–2010, when Wells Fargo alone took in about 14% of applications. It fell to about 20% in 2022 and sits at 22.5% in 2025. Rocket's 2025 share is 5.1%. No single lender today looks like Wells Fargo did in 2009.

3. The lender list itself shrank. About 8,600 institutions received at least one application in 2007. That fell to about 4,300 in 2021, then came back only to about 4,800 in 2025. Fewer doors, and a few hundred of them still do most of the work.

4. The names at the top changed with the crises. In 2007 the list is Countrywide, Wells Fargo, Bank of America, Beneficial, Washington Mutual, and Household Finance. Countrywide is split across two filings that year, Home Loans and Bank, so the corporate family is larger than either line. By 2009 Wells Fargo is alone at the top. Quicken Loans climbs through the 2010s, leads in 2017, and from 2018 the same institution leads most years under the Rocket Mortgage name. United Wholesale Mortgage reaches the top in 2024 and is a close second in 2025.

5. The banks that defined the old list have stepped back from this ranking. Wells Fargo is outside the top 10 by applications in 2023, 2024, and 2025. JPMorgan Chase is outside it from 2022 on. Bank of America is still there, in third place in 2025, with 1.8% of applications.

6. The newer top 10 is a mix of national mortgage companies and specialists. In 2025 the ten are Rocket, United Wholesale, Bank of America, PennyMac, CrossCountry, Newrez, Navy Federal, Mortgage Research Center, Freedom, and 21st Mortgage. This ranking is by applications received, so it is not the same list as a ranking by loans that actually closed. Bank of America ranks higher here than it does on loans made, because a large share of its applications are home-equity and other files that stay on its own books.

7. Filing institutions are not corporate families. Two subsidiaries can appear as two lenders. Combining them would make a few companies look bigger, especially in 2007, when Countrywide's two filings together would have led the list. The tables above leave them separate, the way HMDA filed them.
