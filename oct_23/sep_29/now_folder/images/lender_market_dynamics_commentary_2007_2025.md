# How the mortgage-lending rankings changed, 2007–2025

**Market structure, lender exits, rank movements and the purchase/refinance cycle**  
Prepared 26 September 2026 · Analysis window: 2007–2025, 19 calendar years

This commentary interprets the [annual top-25 lender rankings](top_25_lenders_by_year_2007_2025.md) alongside the new [purchase, refinance and mortgage-rate companion](purchase_refinance_and_rates_2007_2025.md). The companion contains annual industry totals, all 475 top-25 lender purpose breakdowns, mortgage-rate history, and calculations separating changes in loan mix from changes in share within a loan purpose.

**The central finding:** there were several different contests, not one continuous race. The financial crisis removed or absorbed important lenders. Large banks initially inherited business, while independent mortgage companies subsequently expanded. The 2020–2021 refinancing boom rewarded a different business mix from the high-rate market that followed. By 2025, purchase distribution, home-equity lending and acquisitions all mattered alongside refinancing.

## 1. How to read the evidence

**Rank means applications received**, across HMDA action codes 1–5. **Market share means funded-loan count**, action code 1 divided by industry action-code-1 volume. These answer different questions. A lender with many denied or abandoned applications can rank above a lender that funds more loans. “Approved” in the original tables includes approved-but-not-taken applications; those are not loans made and are not included in market share here.

A move from #20 to #5 is an improvement of 15 places. A move from #4 to #32 is a decline of 28 places. Percentages are shares of loan **counts**, not dollars, profits or servicing balances. Changes in share are in **percentage points**, abbreviated pp.

Three levels of evidence are kept separate:

1. **Observed:** counts, ranks, purpose mix and market shares recalculated from the supplied files.
2. **Documented:** dated acquisitions, failures, channel exits and market developments supported by linked sources.
3. **Interpretation:** the mechanism that plausibly connects them. Annual aggregate data cannot identify which competitor supplied a lender's particular new customers or prove that pricing caused a competitor's exit.

Ranks below #25 were checked against all reporting institutions, rather than treating a missing top-25 row as a business closure. Filing entities remain separate unless a comparison explicitly combines named entities. Historical names in the original report sometimes use newer lookup labels; a new label does not necessarily mean a new business.

## 2. The findings that most change the story

- **UWM is a substantial long-run share winner.** Its application rank improved from #15 in 2016 to #2 in 2025, with #1 in 2024. Its share of loans made rose from 0.99% in 2016 to 6.18% in 2025. Its gains extend beyond a favorable purchase/refinance mix.
- **Rocket's post-boom decline is easy to misread.** Its overall share fell from 8.25% in 2021 to 6.29% in 2025, but its share within purchase lending rose from 3.61% to 3.80%, and within refinancing from 11.85% to 13.73%. The shrinking weight of refinancing explains more than its net overall-share decline.
- **Wells Fargo's retreat is deeper than the interest-rate cycle alone.** Its loan count fell 88.9% between 2021 and 2025, versus a 54.5% industry decline. It also lost share within both purchase and refinance lending, alongside a documented strategy to shrink home lending.
- **CrossCountry gained a stronger competitive position in purchase lending.** Its application rank improved from #20 to #5 between 2021 and 2025, while its purchase-market share increased from 1.69% to 2.63%. Its overall loan count still remained below the boom peak.
- **Some apparent disappearances are changes of ownership or reporting identity.** Caliber was integrated into Newrez; Countrywide was acquired by Bank of America; Nationstar/Mr. Cooper remained a large servicing business and was acquired by Rocket in late 2025.
- **Higher rank does not always mean a larger lending operation.** Navy Federal rose from #25 to #7 between 2021 and 2025 while originations fell just 0.8%. Bank of America rose from #10 to #3 while originations fell 48.6%.

All numerical findings in this section come from the local ranking and purpose calculations. The relevant external events are sourced in the sections below.

## 3. The market itself changed dramatically

### 2007–2012: crisis, consolidation and refinancing

Industry loans made fell from 10.44 million in 2007 to 7.18 million in 2008. The crisis also changed who remained available to lend: failures and acquisitions removed brands, transferred customer relationships and consolidated operating capacity. The subsequent market was increasingly refinance-heavy: refinancing accounted for 6.66 million of 9.78 million originations in 2012, about 68.1%.

The 30-year mortgage benchmark averaged 6.34% in 2007 and 3.66% in 2012. Falling borrowing costs gave existing homeowners a reason to replace loans even while purchase activity remained far below 2007. These rate figures are calculated from [Freddie Mac's weekly PMMS observations through FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/MORTGAGE30US).

Wells Fargo's reported entity originated 1.14 million loans in 2012, 11.67% of the local industry total. This was an era in which a large bank could combine a broad franchise with enormous refinance throughput. That position was not permanent.

### 2013–2019: independent lenders become harder to dismiss

Purchase originations increased from 2.74 million in 2012 to 4.25 million in 2017. Refinancing fell from 6.66 million to 2.53 million over those same endpoints. The ranking changes therefore unfolded in a market with a different balance of borrower needs.

Quicken/Rocket reached #1 by applications in 2017, while UWM reached #10. By 2019, Rocket was still #1 and UWM had reached #4. This was more than a name change: independent lenders had become leading origination businesses.

External evidence supports the broader structural shift. The Financial Stability Oversight Council reported that nonbank mortgage companies originated approximately two-thirds of U.S. mortgages in 2022, compared with 39% in 2008. Its report describes both technology strengths and financing/liquidity vulnerabilities in this business model. Those national figures have their own scope and are contextual evidence, not a directly interchangeable denominator for these tables. [Treasury/FSOC, 10 May 2024](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy2331).

Do not interpret the 2017–2018 change as purely economic: HMDA's identifiers, data fields and coverage changed. Cash-out refinancing became separately identifiable, and open-end reporting requirements expanded. [CFPB review of new and revised HMDA fields](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_data-points_updated-review-hmda_report.pdf).

### 2020–2021: refinancing enlarges the market and changes the leaderboard

The local data show 14.54 million originations in 2020 and 15.00 million in 2021. Refinancing alone contributed 8.67 million loans in 2021, 57.8% of all originations. The annual 30-year benchmark averaged 2.96% that year.

The lenders most exposed to this activity had exceptionally large refinance books. In 2021, refinancing represented 83.0% of Rocket's originations, 93.8% of Freedom's, 92.8% of AmeriSave's and 81.7% of Better's. UWM also participated heavily: 64.7% of its originations were refinances.

Those mixes explain why a boom-era ranking should not be treated as a stable measure of purchase-lending strength. It measures successful participation in the market that existed that year, including a large, temporary opportunity to refinance outstanding loans.

### 2022–2025: refinance contraction, rate lock-in and a partial recovery

The annual benchmark rose to 5.34% in 2022 and 6.81% in 2023. The within-year change in 2022 was especially sharp: weekly readings ranged from 3.22% to 7.08%. Industry originations fell **62.0% from 2021 to 2023**.

Purchase lending also suffered. Homeowners with older low-rate mortgages had an incentive to stay in place, limiting resale supply as well as mortgage demand. An FHFA working paper, in its August 2024 revision, estimated that mortgage-rate lock-in prevented 1.72 million sales between 2022 Q2 and 2024 Q2. That is a modeled estimate, not a count observed in this ranking dataset. [FHFA revised working paper](https://www.fhfa.gov/sites/default/files/2024-08/wp2403.pdf).

By 2025, originations had recovered to 6.83 million, up 20.0% from 2023 but still down 54.5% from 2021. The recovery was uneven:

| Segment | 2021 loans made | 2023 loans made | 2025 loans made | 2021–2025 change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Purchase | 5,359,280 | 3,448,703 | 3,592,033 | −33.0% |
| Total refinancing | 8,665,079 | 1,083,700 | 1,995,427 | −77.0% |
| Other purposes | 972,949 | 1,159,323 | 1,240,431 | +27.5% |

“Other” includes home improvement and other reported purposes; it is not a synonym for home equity. However, the product checks show that open-end and junior-lien activity explain much of this category for several prominent banks and specialists.

### UWM: genuine within-segment gains, with a changing source of strength

UWM's rise began before the pandemic. Its originated-loan share increased from 0.99% in 2016 to 1.89% in 2018, 3.64% in 2019 and 6.18% in 2025. The older United Shore/Shore names in the source history refer to the UWM lineage used in the existing report.

The purpose data strengthen the case for competitive gains:

- Purchase originations increased from 230,720 in 2021 to 237,650 in 2025, even as industry purchase lending contracted by a third. UWM's purchase share rose from 4.31% to 6.62%.
- Refinancing fell from 423,471 to 184,470 loans, yet its share of that much smaller segment rose from 4.89% to 9.24%.
- The arithmetic decomposition attributes **+2.92 pp** to changes in share within purposes and **−1.10 pp** to changing industry mix, for a net **+1.82 pp** overall.

The documented strategy is consistent with these gains. UWM's wholesale model distributes through independent mortgage brokers. It launched its “Game On” pricing initiative on 22 June 2022 with the stated goal of gaining share and expanding the broker channel. Its disclosures also emphasized technology and processing speed. These are company-reported strategies, not independent proof that a particular initiative generated a specific number of loans. [UWM Q2 2022 results](https://investors.uwm.com/news/financial-news/news-details/2022/UWM-Holdings-Corporation-Announces-Second-Quarter-2022-Results/default.aspx).

There is an important nuance: UWM's purchase share was **7.11% in 2023**, above its 6.62% in 2025. Its later overall-share improvement was not a straight-line gain in every business. A stronger refinance position and recovering refinance demand also mattered.

### Rocket: less total share, more share inside both major segments

Quicken was #24 by applications in 2007 and #1 in 2017. Quicken Loans formally changed its name to Rocket Mortgage on 31 July 2021; the older and newer labels should not be treated as separate competitive entrants. [Rocket 2021 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1805284/000180528422000011/rkt-20211231.htm).

Rocket's loans made fell from 1,237,033 in 2021 to 429,332 in 2025. Taken alone, that looks like a broad loss of competitive strength. The purpose comparison gives a more precise explanation:

| Rocket measure | 2021 | 2025 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Share of industry purchase loans | 3.61% | 3.80% |
| Share of industry refinance loans | 11.85% | 13.73% |
| Share of all industry loans made | 8.25% | 6.29% |

Rocket remained much more prominent in refinancing than in purchase lending. When refinancing shrank as a proportion of the market, that specialization pulled down its overall share. The decomposition gives **+0.88 pp** from within-purpose changes and **−2.84 pp** from industry mix, producing the observed **−1.96 pp** net change.

Rocket's 2025 acquisitions illustrate an effort to broaden distribution and customer access: Redfin closed on **1 July 2025**, and Mr. Cooper closed on **1 October 2025**. A home-search/agent channel can support purchase acquisition; servicing relationships can support future customer retention and refinancing. Those are plausible strategic mechanisms, not established explanations for the entire year's results. The timing also rules out attributing earlier rank gains to these deals. [Redfin closing announcement](https://ir.rocketcompanies.com/news-and-events/press-releases/press-release-details/2025/Rocket-Companies-Completes-Acquisition-of-Redfin/), [Mr. Cooper closing announcement](https://www.rocketcompanies.com/press-release/rocket-companies-closes-14-2-billion-acquisition-of-mr-cooper/).

The ranking continues to show Rocket Mortgage and Nationstar separately where they file separately. Adding their full-year 2025 counts would not be the same as measuring Rocket's owned production only after the October closing.

### Wells Fargo: a strategic retreat reinforced the cyclical decline

Wells Fargo moved from #1 in 2012 to #4 in 2021, #16 in 2023 and **#32 in 2025**. It did not disappear: its 2025 entity reported 76,355 applications and 41,562 originations. But its share of loans made fell from 11.67% in 2012 to 0.61% in 2025.

The modern purpose comparison confirms a retreat within markets, not merely an unfavorable business mix. Purchase share fell from 1.81% in 2021 to 0.72% in 2025; refinance share fell from 3.16% to 0.64%.

On 10 January 2023, Wells Fargo announced a smaller home-lending business focused on bank customers and minority communities, an exit from correspondent lending and a reduction in its servicing portfolio. This is direct evidence of a strategic decision to reduce breadth. [Wells Fargo announcement](https://newsroom.wf.com/news-releases/news-details/2023/Wells-Fargo-Announces-Strategic-Direction-for-Home-Lending-A-Smaller-Less-Complex-Business-Focused-on-Bank-Customers-and-Minority-Communities/default.aspx).

The correspondent exit is broader context, not a mechanical one-for-one explanation for the reported origination decline: purchased loans are excluded from our action-code-1 measure. Nevertheless, the timing and the losses within both major loan purposes support the interpretation that this was more than a normal refinance downturn.

### CrossCountry, Guild and Veterans United: purchase distribution matters

CrossCountry rose from #49 in 2018 to #20 in 2021 and #5 in 2025. Its purchase originations increased from 90,554 in 2021 to 94,293 in 2025, while industry purchase volume fell. That is stronger evidence of competitive expansion than the rank change alone. The company describes itself as a large distributed retail lender, with a broad branch and loan-officer network. [CrossCountry's 2025 review, published January 2026](https://crosscountrymortgage.com/mortgage/news/ccm-top-retail-mortgage-lender-2026/).

Guild improved from #27 in 2021 to #17 in 2025. Its purchase originations rose from 62,162 to 70,983, and its purchase-market share increased from 1.16% to 1.98%. Acquisition activity is part of the context: in February 2024 it announced the acquisition of Academy Mortgage's retail lending assets. The gain should therefore not be described as entirely organic. [Guild announcement](https://www.guildmortgage.com/about-us/news/press-release/guild-mortgage-strategically-expands-with-acquisition-of-academy-mortgage/).

Mortgage Research Center, operating as [Veterans United](https://careers.veteransunited.com/applicant-resources/), rose from #19 to #8 between 2021 and 2025. Purchase loans were 82.6% of its 2025 originations; 76,057 of its 82,764 total originations were VA loans. Its purchase-market share rose from 1.44% to 1.90%, despite its absolute purchase count declining. This demonstrates the value of a distinct borrower/product franchise, while leaving open how much came from service, distribution, pricing or competitor withdrawal.

### loanDepot, Freedom, AmeriSave and Better: different degrees of refinance exposure

loanDepot fell from #5 in 2021 to #11 in 2025, with originations down 76.5%. Unlike Rocket, it lost share inside both purchase and refinance lending. Its 2022 results identified lower market volumes and the exit from wholesale lending as important business changes. That exit reduced the distribution channels through which it competed. [loanDepot full-year 2022 results](https://investors.loandepot.com/news/corporate-and-financial-news/corporate-and-financial-news-details/2023/loanDepot-announces-year-end-and-fourth-quarter-2022-financial-results/default.aspx).

Freedom's path was more cyclical: #3 in 2021, #34 in 2023 and #9 in 2025. Its refinance-heavy 2021 book made the rate shock particularly consequential. Originations recovered from 22,739 in 2023 to 69,982 in 2025, but remained 80.6% below 2021. Re-entry into the top ten is a recovery from the trough, not restoration of its boom-era scale.

AmeriSave moved from #6 to #24 between 2021 and 2025. Refinancing was 92.8% of its 2021 originations and 97.0% in 2025. A shrinking refinance market is central to its decline, though its share of that segment also fell from 1.37% to 1.18%.

Better fell from #17 in 2021 to #116 in 2023, then recovered to #44 in 2025. Its 14,567 originations in 2025 establish that falling outside the top 25 was not equivalent to exiting. Its refinance-market share fell from 1.26% to 0.14% between 2021 and 2025, so market mix alone cannot explain the contraction. The data do not isolate operational execution, cost reductions, distribution changes or other causes; those would require a separate company-level investigation.

### Banks and specialists whose rank rose without comparable volume growth

Bank of America improved from #10 in 2021 to #3 in 2025, but funded almost half as many loans. Its purchase share and refinance share both slipped slightly. In 2025, 56,653 of its 88,530 originated records were open-end credit. Its high application rank therefore describes a broader housing-credit business, not third place in conventional first-lien purchase lending.

Navy Federal is an even clearer example of resilience and product change. Total originations were nearly unchanged at 81,181 in 2021 and 80,547 in 2025. Yet purchase plus refinance originations fell, while other purposes rose from 920 to 37,295. Of those 37,295 other-purpose originations in 2025, 34,052 were junior-lien loans. Its rise is not simply “a purchase lender won customers”; a different product mix helped sustain volume.

21st Mortgage rose from #24 to #10 while total originations fell 8.3%. Its reported 2025 loans were overwhelmingly manufactured housing: 27,961 of 28,058 originations. Vanderbilt's 2025 book was also purchase-heavy, although its manufactured-housing proportion was lower, about 59.9%. These firms compete in materially different product markets from a typical conventional refinance specialist.

Spring EQ illustrates actual expansion in a smaller specialty: originations rose from 9,983 in 2021 to 32,174 in 2025, with 97.7% of the latter secured by junior liens. Coastal Community Bank's #25 application rank in 2025 similarly needs product context: 21,221 of its 21,244 originations were open-end, and only 15 were reported as home-purchase loans. These observations identify business mix, not the cause of their growth or a named source of customers.

### Later consolidations and withdrawals

**Caliber → Newrez.** New Residential completed its Caliber acquisition on 23 August 2021. Newrez's corporate history dates completion of Caliber integration to 2023. Caliber's separate application rank fell from #8 in 2021 to #98 in 2023; its old LEI has no action-1–5 records in the supplied 2025 file. [Acquisition completion filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1556593/000114036121029131/brhc10028278_8k.htm), [Newrez history](https://www.newrez.com/about-us/).

Newrez's own row improves from #11 in 2021 to #6 in 2025, but that is not a clean standalone growth comparison. Newrez and Caliber together had 368,866 originations in 2021, 2.46% of industry, versus 66,567 under the Newrez identifier in 2025, 0.97%. That combined-origin comparison is a useful sensitivity check, not a complete corporate-production reconciliation: other entities, acquired assets and purchased loans may sit outside those two IDs.

**Ditech → acquired assets.** Newrez's history records the acquisition of selected Ditech mortgage assets in 2019. Ditech's disappearance as an independent row should therefore be analyzed partly as consolidation, not simply as demand won away one application at a time. The source supports an asset transaction, not the claim that every Ditech obligation or operation transferred to Newrez. [Newrez history](https://www.newrez.com/about-us/).

**Home Point → two different destinations.** Home Point was #15 in 2021, with 209,325 originations. In 2023, origination assets were sold to The Loan Store, while Mr. Cooper acquired Home Point Capital and its remaining servicing-related business. The origination transaction and servicing acquisition must not be collapsed into “all its new loans went to Mr. Cooper.” [Seller's transaction counsel on the May 2023 sale](https://www.jonesday.com/en/practices/experience/2023/05/home-point-capital-sells-wholesale-originations-business-to-the-loan-store), [Mr. Cooper 2023 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/933136/000093313624000017/coop-20231231.htm).

**Discover → actual home-loan exit in 2025.** Discover was #4 by applications in both 2023 and 2024, yet was #36 in 2025 with 9,711 originations. Discover states that it announced closure of its home-loan business in July 2025. The 2025 count is consequently a transition-year observation, not a normal full-year competitive run rate. [Discover home-loan notice](https://www.discover.com/home-loans/).

**Nationstar/Mr. Cooper → still present, then acquired.** Nationstar was #30 by applications in 2025, with 52,217 originations. Its large servicing business is not measured by that origination rank. Rocket's October 2025 acquisition is a documented ownership change, not evidence that the entity had stopped operating when it fell outside the top 25. [Rocket closing announcement](https://www.rocketcompanies.com/press-release/rocket-companies-closes-14-2-billion-acquisition-of-mr-cooper/).

## 6. Who took whose market?

### What can be quantified

Between 2021 and 2025, UWM gained 1.82 pp of overall originated-loan share and CrossCountry gained 0.79 pp. Wells Fargo lost 1.90 pp, loanDepot 1.25 pp, Freedom 1.38 pp and Rocket 1.96 pp. These are changes in positions in a market whose size and product mix changed substantially.

They do **not** establish that UWM's gain came specifically from Rocket, Wells or Home Point. In fact, Rocket and UWM both increased their share of purchase and refinance originations over these endpoints. Together their overall originated-loan share was 12.61% in 2021 and 12.47% in 2025—nearly unchanged, even as UWM gained ground relative to Rocket in the overall ranking.

The strongest evidence of gaining ground from the rest of a segment is a rise in that segment's share. UWM, CrossCountry and Guild achieved this in purchase lending; UWM and Rocket did so in refinancing. Naming the particular displaced lender would require borrower-level switching evidence, channel-level production, geographic overlap or loan-officer/team migration data.

### The plausible transfer mechanisms

| Change | Who had an opportunity to gain? | Mechanism and strength of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wells Fargo shrank home lending | Other purchase and refinance lenders, including banks and nonbanks | Strategic retreat is documented; lost segment share is observed. Exact recipients are not identified. |
| loanDepot left wholesale; Home Point sold origination assets | Continuing wholesale lenders and The Loan Store | Channel exit and asset transfer are documented. UWM's strategy and share gains are consistent with benefiting from channel reallocation, but the data cannot allocate the gain to either competitor. |
| Refinancing collapsed | Purchase-heavy lenders became more prominent in the overall mix | Strong aggregate evidence. Much “share loss” is disappearing refinance demand, not a customer transferring to another lender. |
| Retail networks expanded or acquired lending teams/assets | CrossCountry, Guild and other purchase lenders | Purchase-share gains are observed; Guild's acquisition is documented. Organic and acquired gains are not separately measured here. |
| Homeowners retained older first mortgages | Junior-lien and open-end providers | FHFA documents lock-in; the local product data show relevant specialist growth. The causal customer pathway remains an inference. |
| Rocket acquired Redfin and Mr. Cooper | Rocket's broader corporate platform | Ownership changes are documented. Future distribution and retention benefits are strategic possibilities, not proven full-year 2025 share gains. |

The economically useful conclusion is that **distribution and product fit determined where surviving demand could go**, while the rate shock removed a large amount of demand altogether. Acquisition transferred some businesses explicitly; other changes redistributed opportunity more diffusely.

## 7. Additional industry dynamics behind the rankings

### Home equity became more relevant when replacing a first mortgage was expensive

A homeowner with a low-rate first mortgage may prefer additional junior-lien borrowing over refinancing the full balance at a higher rate. This is an economic interpretation consistent with rate lock-in, not something the aggregate records reveal about an individual borrower's decision.

The local evidence is concrete: Spring EQ's 2025 originations were 97.7% junior lien; Bank of America's were 64.0% open-end; Navy Federal's other-purpose originations were predominantly junior lien. That helps explain why “all mortgage applications” and “home-purchase mortgage competition” increasingly produce different leaderboards.

### Builders have a distribution and incentive advantage of their own

DHI Mortgage was #22 by applications in 2025, and 69,140 of its 69,159 originations were purchase loans. Builder-affiliated lending operates at the point where the home is sold, and builders can use financing incentives to support sales. D.R. Horton's fiscal 2025 annual report describes increased mortgage-rate buydowns among its incentives. This supports a distinct competitive mechanism, although its fiscal-year disclosures do not exactly align with calendar-year HMDA. [D.R. Horton 2025 annual report](https://investor.drhorton.com/~/media/Files/D/D-R-Horton-IR/documents/quarterly-reports/2025-dhi-annual-report.pdf).

### Servicing scale and purchased-loan scale are separate from origination rank

PennyMac Loan Services rose from #9 in 2021 to #4 in 2025 by applications. Its 2025 HMDA filing also records **293,228 purchased loans**, separate from its 100,816 originations. Those purchased records are intentionally excluded from this report's loans-made share. A corporate production ranking that includes correspondent purchases can therefore differ sharply from this leaderboard.

Likewise, a servicing acquisition buys responsibility for an existing mortgage book and access to customer relationships. It does not create an equal number of new loans in the acquisition year. That distinction is essential for interpreting Mr. Cooper, Newrez and Rocket.

### Larger individual winners do not prove that this measure became more concentrated

The institutions ranked in the top 25 by applications accounted for 40.80% of industry originations in 2021, versus 35.30% in 2025. This is the share held by an **application-ranked group**, not a formal top-25-by-originations concentration measure and not a parent-company concentration index.

It cautions against telling a simple story that “a few giants captured everything.” Leading platforms gained in some segments while activity also spread among specialists and lenders outside this selected group. A full concentration study would consolidate parent ownership and calculate segment-specific concentration consistently over time.

## 8. Conclusions the data does not support

**Do not interpret loanDepot's #1 application rank in 2016 as #1 in funded mortgages.** It reported 823,096 applications and 133,427 originations that year. In 2017, applications fell to 238,288 while originations were almost unchanged at 134,968. That divergence is a warning about application composition or reporting, not proof of a one-year collapse in its funded business. The specific cause has not been established here.

**Do not call every missing name a failure.** Wells Fargo, Better, Freedom and Nationstar all provide examples of lenders operating below the top-25 cutoff in particular years. Caliber provides an integration example; Discover provides a documented business exit.

**Do not infer underwriting quality from raw approval rates.** A lender's product mix, applicant pool, application process, withdrawals and approved-but-not-taken outcomes affect those rates. High conversion is not, by itself, evidence of better credit decisions or better customer outcomes.

**Do not claim causation from two lines moving in opposite directions.** The purpose decomposition improves the comparison, but it does not control for geography, loan amount, borrower credit, lien, channel, acquisition scope, profitability or reporting changes.

## 9. Overall assessment

The biggest long-run transition is from a crisis-era field containing Countrywide, WaMu, HFC/Beneficial and other now-absent names toward a field led in application volume by independent platforms such as Rocket and UWM. Wells Fargo exemplifies the reversal from post-crisis dominance to a deliberately narrower role.

Within the recent cycle, **UWM's gains are supported by stronger shares inside both purchase and refinancing**, while **Rocket's overall-share decline substantially reflects the contraction of the segment where it is strongest**. CrossCountry and Guild provide evidence of stronger purchase positions. Freedom and AmeriSave remain particularly sensitive to refinance activity. Navy Federal, Bank of America and home-equity specialists show how a broader product mix changes an all-purpose ranking.

The most informative way to read the leaderboard is therefore to ask three questions together: **Did the loan count grow? Did share within the same loan purpose grow? Did the business itself change through acquisition, exit or product expansion?** The companion file supplies those controls before attaching a competitive story to a rank movement.
